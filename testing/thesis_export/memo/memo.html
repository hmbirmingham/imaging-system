Blind validation of an automated colony counter

What held up, what didn't, and why

Manual colony counting is slow, and it gives you a number with no context. A tech counts a plate, writes down 47, and that's it: no flag on the odd satellite colony near the edge, no record of which colonies were touching, no repeatability across shifts. Existing automated counters fix the speed problem but mostly stop there. You get a count, not an assessment.

This year I've been building an instrument that does more: automated bacterial colony counting with morphological anomaly detection and an organism-aware profile system, in Dr. Eric Ryberg's lab as a CAPS Research Scholar at UConn. This is a validation memo: I ran the system blind against synthetic data it had never seen, and I'm reporting the real numbers, including the one that still misses our target.

Revision 1 · 13 October 2026. After publishing this memo I found a third bug: contour extraction was discarding colony splits the segmentation stage had already made correctly. Fixing it moved pooled F1 from 0.869 to 0.918, clearing the target, and a fourth of five sets now passes. It also cost something real — precision is no longer 1.000. Figures below are corrected, with the published value shown as was. Where the original drew a conclusion I now think was wrong, I say so and leave the original claim visible.

System architecture

The full stack, built from the register level up:

  • Hardware. Custom power electronics on a KiCad-designed PCB, driving plate illumination and imaging.
  • Vision. An OpenCV computer-vision pipeline on a Raspberry Pi 5, running from illumination correction through segmentation to morphometric analysis.
  • Anomaly detection. Two layers: geometric flagging (area, circularity, and aspect-ratio outliers) plus a statistical/ML layer.
  • Organism profiles. Counting and flagging thresholds adapt per organism instead of applying one fixed set of assumptions to every plate.

Blind validation methodology

I generated 250 synthetic plates across five held-out parameter sets (density, illumination, size variance, morphology, and touching rate), 50 plates per set, using combinations never seen during pipeline development. Ground truth (exact colony positions, radii, and anomaly labels) was embedded in the generator output.

Critically: quantify_colonies() was called with only the image path. Ground truth was loaded strictly afterward, only to score what the pipeline had already returned. It never informed detection. That ordering is what makes this validation blind.

Matching used one-to-one nearest-centroid assignment with a gate at 1.5× the ground-truth colony's radius, not raw count comparison. Otherwise a false positive and a missed colony can cancel out and look like a perfect score. Results are pooled (micro-averaged) across all plates in a set rather than averaged per plate, so a handful of near-empty plates can't swing the aggregate.

I benchmarked against peer-reviewed numbers for commercial and open-source counters, not vendor claims: OpenCFU at 50.31% mean count error in an independent re-benchmark (Chen et al. 2025), and ColonyDoc-It at 18.3–59.7% depending on agar medium (Heuser et al. 2023).

Results

Overall pooled F1 across all 250 plates was 0.918, against a target of 0.90. Four of the five held-out sets passed. One did not. (Previously reported: 0.869, with three of five passing.)

The precision claim has to be withdrawn

The original memo put weight on one number above all the others: precision was 1.000 on every set and every plate, so the pipeline never fabricated a colony. That is no longer true, and the reason is the fix itself.

Precision is now 0.995. Across 250 plates the pipeline produced 41 false colonies, against 7,683 correct detections and 1,339 still missed. They are not evenly spread: 30 of the 41 sit on irregular_morphology, the set whose colonies are deliberately star-shaped, where watershed over-splits a single concave colony into fragments. Before the fix those fragments were being silently merged back together by the very bug I was fixing, which is the only reason precision ever read 1.000 there. That set's recall went to a perfect 1.000 in exchange. dense_pack contributes 9, high_touching 2, and the remaining two sets none.

So the honest version of the original claim is narrower: the pipeline rarely invents colonies, and when it does it is splitting a real one rather than hallucinating from noise. That is a meaningfully weaker statement than the one I published, and the trade bought 0.083 of pooled recall.

Mean count error was 11.43% pooled across all five sets and 8.89% on the four passing sets (previously 15.75% and 5.31% on three). Both remain below the published error rates above (18.3–59.7%). Those benchmarks were measured on real agar and mine on synthetic plates, so this is context, not a head-to-head comparison. A direct comparison requires running both systems on identical plates under identical conditions, which is what biological validation is for.

Why one set still fails

When two colonies overlap heavily enough, the merged blob's distance transform has only one local maximum, and there is no second peak for watershed — or any marker-seeding technique — to find. I verified this directly rather than inferring it from the F1 drop, and I have since quantified it across all 2,342 overlapping ground-truth pairs in the held-out sets.

For two overlapping disks of radii r₁ ≥ r₂ whose centres are distance d apart, two peaks survive when d² > r₁² − r₂² — the intersection chord falls between the two centres rather than beyond the smaller one. That holds for 68.4% of overlapping pairs here.

As a necessary condition it is excellent: of the 739 pairs that fail it, 99.6% go unresolved. As an explanation of the remaining gap it is not sufficient, and this is where the original memo overreached. Pairs that satisfy it still resolve only 39.4% of the time, and the condition accounts for just 43.1% of unresolved pairs. Most unresolved overlaps are geometrically splittable and still don't get split.

What explains more of the rest is that overlaps are frequently not pairwise at all. Clusters here run to nine colonies, and among pairs the geometry says are splittable, resolution degrades steadily as the surrounding cluster grows: 51.5% for an isolated pair, 37.3% at three colonies, 29.1% at four, 26.8% at five or more. Of the unresolved-but-splittable pairs, 70.5% sit in clusters of three or more. A two-circle model is simply the wrong unit for most of what remains.

I previously wrote that closing this gap needs a different segmentation approach entirely, such as Hough-circle fitting or concave-point contour splitting. That is still true, but only of what remains after the extraction bug — a substantially smaller gap than the one I was describing, and one where multi-colony clustering, not pairwise geometry, is the thing to attack.

For perspective: the FDA BAM countable range (25–250 CFU/plate) has an upper limit because crowded plates "may be difficult to count or may inhibit the growth of some bacteria" — for humans too. Yet dense_pack's 80–120 colonies per plate sit well inside that countable range and still cost measurable recall. It's a known industry-wide hard case, not a naive implementation gap. It's still a gap, and I'm reporting it, not excusing it.

Bugs found and fixed along the way

Three meaningful bugs were found and fixed. I'm listing them because reproducible research means documenting what changed, not just what the final system does.

  • Per-blob watershed threshold. Watershed's foreground threshold was computed against the whole plate's peak distance value instead of per blob, silently dropping markers on mixed-size plates. Fixing it moved F1 from 0.804 to 0.872 (measured before the area fix below).
  • A regression from that fix. The fix undersized isolated colonies' measured area by about 10%. A separate area-accuracy CI check caught it, and it was fixed before the branch merged.
  • Watershed splits thrown away at contour extraction. This is the one that moved everything, and I found it after this memo was already published, while tracing deep-saddle touching pairs stage by stage to see why they weren't splitting. They were splitting. I was collapsing every watershed region into one binary mask and calling findContours on it once — and findContours traces 8-connected foreground, while watershed separates regions with a line one pixel wide. Two regions it had correctly split stayed diagonally connected around that line and came back as a single contour. Measured before the fix: 14.9% of watershed regions lost on high_touching, 11.1% on dense_pack, against 0.3% on the sparse sets — concentrated, almost perfectly, in the two sets I had been reporting as failures. That concentration is exactly why it hid: it looked like the segmentation ceiling I thought I had already proven. Tracing contours per watershed label fixed it. Two iterations of this project were spent making watershed produce better splits; it had been producing them the whole time, and I was discarding them one line later.

Anomaly detection: still deferred

Anomaly detection is weak across the board (pooled F1 of 0.405, previously 0.234) and I still have not tuned it. The improvement is incidental: colonies that are finally split correctly also receive their touching-colony flag correctly, which that layer had been missing wholesale. Counting has now converged on four of five sets, so this is the next thing to actually work on rather than defer.

What's next

The quickest win. 99.2% of size_variance's missed colonies exceed a fixed max_area_mm2 filter. Deriving that limit from plate calibration, not a hardcoded constant, should close most of that gap fast.

Cluster-aware splitting. Now the largest identified source of remaining loss, ahead of pairwise geometry.

Biological validation. The next major phase moves to real clinical plates instead of synthetic renders. Everything above is synthetic by design, so the pipeline still has to prove itself against real agar, real bubbles, and real staining before any of these numbers mean anything clinically. That's the next memo.
